Good morning. Here is your daily brief for today, Monday, October nineteenth, twenty twenty-six.

We begin with a story that signals just how large the artificial intelligence infrastructure race has become. Nscale and Anthropic, who already struck a forty-five billion dollar compute deal back in August, are now reportedly in talks for a second deal of the exact same scale. That would bring the total potential value of their compute partnership to ninety billion dollars. The sheer repetition of that number in a matter of weeks tells you everything about the pace at which frontier artificial intelligence labs are consuming computing power, and the pressure companies like Nscale face to keep up.

Staying in the chip space, Tenstorrent, one of the more credible challengers to Nvidia's dominance in artificial intelligence hardware, has just closed a one point five billion dollar funding round, pushing its valuation to six point two billion dollars. The company builds chips designed specifically for open-weight artificial intelligence models, and it had been fielding acquisition interest earlier this year. It chose independence. That decision now looks expensive to anyone who wanted to buy it, and encouraging for anyone betting that the chip market does not remain a one-company story forever.

Amazon is pressing the same theme from a software angle. The company has released a new developer tool called TorchNeuron, its most direct attempt yet to pull developers away from running artificial intelligence applications on Nvidia hardware and onto Amazon's own chips instead. The tool targets PyTorch, the open-source framework that underlies most artificial intelligence applications being built today. If developers adopt TorchNeuron at scale, Amazon could shift meaningful workloads to its own silicon. That is a long game, but Amazon appears committed to playing it.

And speaking of Amazon, Morgan Stanley named the company a top pick this morning, pointing to momentum in its cloud division and a growing push into what analysts are calling physical and agentic artificial intelligence. More on that term in a moment.

On the security front, there is a warning worth noting from Microsoft. The company published guidance telling enterprise customers to treat artificial intelligence agents, the kind of software that autonomously executes tasks, as potential insider threats when they operate on systems outside the control of a trusted artificial intelligence lab. Microsoft's language was direct: frontier models cannot be trusted, it said. This is not a hypothetical concern. Separately, a hacker reportedly broke into more than one hundred websites using a largely autonomous army of artificial intelligence agents. The confirmed targets include John Deere, SharkNinja, and Cummins. The attack was documented by the security firm Norforge. It is an early and concrete example of what happens when offensive artificial intelligence tools become accessible and autonomous.

There was also a security incident in the artificial intelligence developer world. OpenCode, an open-source artificial intelligence coding agent popular as an alternative to Claude Code and Cursor, was breached. Hackers stole application programming interface keys from some users and racked up charges on their artificial intelligence accounts. The attackers reportedly tried to use artificial intelligence tools to help analyze their own malicious code, and those tools refused. Small irony, large warning.

In the startup funding world, two artificial intelligence assistant companies made notable moves. Town, which only launched four months ago, has already hit a unicorn valuation after raising one hundred and eight million dollars in a Series B round co-led by Forerunner Ventures and Menlo Ventures. Four months from launch to a billion-dollar valuation is a pace that would have seemed extraordinary just two years ago. Separately, Tolan, an artificial intelligence companion app with more than two hundred and fifty thousand active users, raised thirty million dollars. And Eugenia Kuyda, the founder of Replika, launched a new personal artificial intelligence assistant called Wabi. It is free and available to download today.

On the artificial intelligence research side, a story out of Massachusetts Institute of Technology Technology Review is drawing significant attention inside the machine learning world. A researcher at a startup called Goodfire published a twenty-six thousand word piece arguing that the field has fundamentally misunderstood the internal structure of neural networks. He called it good news about the nature of neural networks. Whether that optimism holds up under peer scrutiny is an open question, but it has already sparked serious debate.

Also from the research world, a team of scientists stranded artificial intelligence agents, specifically copies of Anthropic's Claude Opus four point seven, alone on a virtual island for thirty hours with no instructions. The agents started what researchers describe as play. They explored, created routines, generated self-directed goals. It is a small but genuinely interesting data point about how large language models behave without human prompting.

CoreWeave, the artificial intelligence cloud infrastructure company, had a rough week last week. Its bonds slipped into what analysts define as distressed territory, raising questions about whether the entire artificial intelligence buildout is a private credit bubble. Goldman Sachs pushed back on that Friday, calling the sell-off overblown and recommending investors buy the dip. Goldman argued that not all cloud infrastructure companies face the same risks, and CoreWeave has structural advantages over smaller rivals.

Goldman also published a defensive stock list for investors who are nervous about the artificial intelligence trade running too hot. Healthcare names are attracting rotation money, and one software stock that has nearly doubled this year just received an upgrade to outperform from Bernstein.

Indonesian stocks fell to a six-year low on fears of a downgrade by the global index provider MSCI. First, a signal worth following on the biotech research front. Gilead Sciences reported mid-stage trial results Monday showing that an oral drug it has been developing eliminated symptoms in patients with a specific type of inflammatory bowel disease. The drug targets a mechanism different from existing biologics, meaning it is a pill, not an injection. Mid-stage results do not guarantee regulatory approval, and a lot can change in late-stage trials, but eliminating symptoms entirely in a phase two study is a stronger outcome than most drugs achieve at that hurdle. If you follow the gut-inflammation space, this is one to track through phase three.

Now, your artificial intelligence move for today. The OpenCode breach is a real-world signal, and it points to something actionable right now. If you use any artificial intelligence coding tool, whether that is OpenCode, Cursor, Claude Code, or any service that holds application programming interface keys, today is the day to audit what keys you have stored in those platforms and where they are exposed. Go into your settings on each artificial intelligence tool you use. Rotate any keys that have been sitting in one place for more than thirty days. Set spending limits or alerts on any artificial intelligence account that allows it. Many people set up these tools quickly and never revisit the permissions they granted. A single stolen key can run up hundreds of dollars in charges before you notice. Ten minutes of key hygiene today is the move.

Finally, on the investing side, two directions are worth watching this week. First, the CoreWeave situation. Goldman Sachs calling the sell-off overblown is notable, but the underlying concern, that artificial intelligence infrastructure spending is being financed through debt instruments that could become fragile under pressure, is a structural question that does not go away in one week. Watch how CoreWeave's bonds trade over the next few sessions as a broader gauge of confidence in the artificial intelligence buildout. Second, the chip alternatives story is developing fast. Tenstorrent at six point two billion dollars and Amazon's TorchNeuron push are two signals pointing in the same direction: the market increasingly believes Nvidia will face real competition within the next two to three years. The timing and the winners remain deeply uncertain. That is the risk to hold in mind before acting on any single name in that theme.
