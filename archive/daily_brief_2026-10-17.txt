Good morning. Here is your daily brief for today, Friday, October seventeenth.

Let us start with tech.

Meta has reversed course on language in its commercial terms that sparked significant backlash earlier this week. The updated terms had contained wording that suggested Meta could scrape content from websites, including news publishers, to train its artificial intelligence systems. After a wave of criticism from media organizations and digital rights advocates, Meta removed that language entirely. A spokesperson insisted the change did not reflect a shift in actual practice, saying the original wording was simply meant to clarify existing data-use policies. Critics are not entirely satisfied. The underlying question, how much of the open web can a technology company legally consume to train its models, remains deeply unresolved. Watch for this fight to continue in courts and in legislatures.

Replit has now officially confirmed that Open A I is replacing Anthropic as its default model provider. Chief executive Amjad Masad clarified over the weekend that while Claude will remain available to customers on the platform, Open A I models will power the default experience going forward. This follows the earlier signal we reported about the two companies parting ways. For Open A I, landing Replit as a flagship partner is a meaningful win in the competitive coding-tool space.

Anthropic itself is in the news for different reasons. The company says its workforce composition has changed substantially over the past year. The company is hiring fewer junior employees and more senior specialists, and internal data suggests staff are spending significantly more time in meetings than they were twelve months ago. Analysts read this as a sign that Anthropic is maturing fast, moving from a scrappy research outfit into a structured enterprise with all the organizational complexity that entails. Separately, Anthropic's Jack Clark testified before policymakers, arguing that artificial intelligence is improving at a pace that could vastly expand United States economic output. Clark called for international cooperation and, in his words, unusual and serious rules to govern the most transformative versions of the technology before they arrive.

On the artificial intelligence infrastructure side, websites are beginning to actively block autonomous A I agents. Autonomous agents, the kind offered by Open A I, Meta, Google, and others, are designed to browse the internet on your behalf, handling tasks like booking reservations or managing your inbox. But a growing number of website operators are updating their terms of service and technical configurations specifically to deny access to these agents. The response is partly economic, publishers and retailers worry about losing traffic and control over their content, and partly legal, with liability questions still unresolved. This is an emerging friction point that could meaningfully shape how useful agent-based A I actually becomes in practice.

South Korea's finance minister offered a steadying message on chips, telling investors that the semiconductor market is expected to remain solid at least through next year and into two thousand twenty-seven. Supply remains tight globally even as a broader chip sell-off pressured valuations last week. That backdrop is worth keeping in mind as earnings season for major semiconductor companies begins.

Speaking of markets, artificial intelligence stocks broadly fell last week as inflation data came in hotter than expected, rattling a trade that had run hard through much of this year. Bank of America analysts are flagging select technology names as buying opportunities ahead of their quarterly earnings, arguing the sell-off is creating an entry point rather than a fundamental shift. Separately, Berkshire Hathaway has made a notable investment in Bending Spoons, a software consolidator that only went public a few months ago. Berkshire's backing gives the Italian company significant credibility as it continues acquiring and restructuring legacy software businesses. Timberland is also drawing unusual institutional attention, with pension funds acquiring large tracts of forest across the United States as an ultra-long-horizon inflation hedge. Returns can take decades and the risk is real, but the appetite is growing.

Now to world news.

France experienced its most serious civil unrest in years. More than half a million people took to the streets across the country to protest the cost of living. By evening, demonstrations turned violent in multiple cities. Cars and buildings were set alight, shops were looted, and more than a thousand people were arrested. The government is under pressure to respond substantively, not just with police deployments.

President Trump has waived a broad package of Russia sanctions that Congress passed in September. Ukrainian President Zelenskyy condemned the decision in direct terms, calling it simply wrong. Trump cited national security considerations for the waiver. The move intensifies an already strained relationship between Washington and Kyiv at a moment when the war's trajectory remains uncertain.

And on a more human note, the Pope has been discharged from hospital following lung cancer surgery. He is expected to give the traditional Angelus prayer in Saint Peter's Square on Sunday. The Vatican says his recovery is proceeding well.

Starting with health research.

A study published this week in a peer-reviewed journal examined how G L P one receptor agonist therapy, the class of drugs that includes medications like semaglutide, is being delivered through digital-first platforms specifically in women. The research, led by a team including cardiologist John Deanfield, looked at real-world outcomes when patients access these medications through telehealth and app-based services rather than traditional clinical settings. The signal is meaningful. Women in the study who received G L P one therapy through digital platforms showed strong adherence rates and meaningful weight reduction outcomes, comparable to those seen in conventional clinic-based trials. What this tells us is that the barrier of access, finding a doctor, getting a referral, navigating insurance, may be dropping fast. Digital delivery is not just a convenience play. It may be genuinely broadening who benefits from this class of treatment. Separately, researchers are also taking a harder look at oral formulations of obesity drugs, specifically for patients who avoid injectable therapies. Early pharmacological data suggests oral G L P one options are becoming more viable, though they remain behind injectables in efficacy profiles for now. The takeaway for you: if G L P one options have been something you or someone you care for has considered but avoided because of logistics or needles, the landscape is changing quickly and worth a fresh conversation with a physician.

Now, artificial intelligence, your move today.

With websites actively blocking A I agents as we covered this morning, here is something worth doing today. Go to your browser and test an A I agent on a site you actually use regularly. Open A I's operator mode or similar tools from competing providers will show you firsthand where agent access is already breaking down. Notice which sites cooperate and which ones return errors or stripped-down results. Then take ten minutes to map out which two or three tasks in your weekly workflow could theoretically be handed to an agent if the access problem were solved. This is not a passive exercise. Building that map now puts you ahead of the curve when the access friction resolves, and it will resolve as companies negotiate arrangements with major platforms. Understanding where agents work today and where they do not is the foundation for knowing how to integrate them into serious work. Do not wait for the tool to be perfect. Understand the landscape now.

Finally, investing, where to look.

Two directions worth watching this week. First, software consolidators. Berkshire Hathaway's quiet but significant bet on Bending Spoons signals that institutional money sees value in companies that acquire and operationally restructure legacy software businesses. This is a category that has been overlooked during the pure artificial intelligence growth rush. If the A I trade continues to cool, money may rotate toward profitable, cash-generating software businesses that have real customers and real margins. The risk is execution. Consolidation strategies depend heavily on management discipline and integration skill, and not every acquirer delivers. Second, semiconductors. South Korea's finance minister's comment about supply remaining tight through two thousand twenty-seven is worth taking seriously as a directional signal, not a guarantee. The chip sell-off last week may have created breathing room to look at names with real demand visibility tied to data center build-outs. As always, this is a category with significant geopolitical exposure, particularly around Taiwan, and that risk belongs in any assessment you make.
