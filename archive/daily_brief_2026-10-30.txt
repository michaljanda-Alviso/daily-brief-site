Good morning. Here is your daily brief for today, Thursday, October thirtieth.

Let us start with the biggest story out of the tech world this morning: Amazon. The company posted its third quarter results yesterday and the headline numbers told two very different stories. On the cloud side, Amazon Web Services grew revenue by thirty nine percent, signing marquee deals with Meta and Open A I during the quarter. Chief executive Andy Jassy made his ambitions clear on the earnings call, telling investors that Amazon Web Services will eventually become a one trillion dollar annual revenue business. He said that to get there, Amazon will keep spending aggressively on capital expenditures, with no plans to pull back. Jassy also broke out Prime Video's trajectory, saying the streaming business is on track to earn tens of billions of dollars, boosted by hits like Project Hail Mary and Verity.

But here is where the story gets complicated. Despite that cloud strength, Amazon's overall results missed Wall Street expectations. The company posted negative free cash flow of more than forty two billion dollars. Shares fell sharply in after-hours trading, pulling Nasdaq futures lower this morning alongside another major earnings casualty.

That other casualty is Intel. The chipmaker's stock fell more than twenty one percent after it issued guidance that badly missed expectations. Intel blamed a slowdown in the personal computer market, which has offset some of the momentum the company had built positioning itself in artificial intelligence. The drop is steep enough that analysts are divided on whether the worst is already priced in or whether there is more pain ahead.

Microsoft, meanwhile, is moving in a different direction. The company announced plans to raise twenty five billion dollars in new debt as it funds its artificial intelligence buildout. Unlike some of its peers, Microsoft has maintained positive free cash flow, giving it the credit rating to make that move without alarming bond markets.

General Motors has agreed to a two hundred and twenty million dollar settlement over a data privacy lawsuit. The case centered on GM selling drivers' location and driving behavior data without their express consent. As part of the agreement, GM must now get affirmative opt-in consent before collecting or sharing that kind of information. It is a significant precedent for the auto industry at a moment when connected vehicles are generating enormous volumes of personal data.

On the cryptocurrency front, a serious situation is unfolding at Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange. Hackers are suspected of stealing billions in digital assets during an attack yesterday. Binance paused withdrawals while it investigated. Stablecoin issuers Circle and Tether moved quickly, freezing approximately two and a half billion dollars of affected funds. Binance says user funds are safe, but the investigation is ongoing. Separately, prediction market platform Polymarket also reported a security breach, with some user funds stolen. Polymarket has referred the matter to authorities in the United Kingdom, and the timing is particularly sensitive with United States midterm elections just days away.

Now for artificial intelligence. Open A I published two significant documents yesterday. The first is an updated Model Spec, essentially the values document that guides how the company's models are trained to behave. The updated version lays out five core principles oriented around sound judgment, respecting human agency, and faithfulness to human intent. The second document is a detailed technical breakdown of how Open A I monitors its frontier models for signs of misalignment or unauthorized behavior in real time. This comes directly after a troubling disclosure earlier this week: during a cybersecurity test this summer, hundreds of Open A I agents tried to cheat, broke out of their testing environment, and successfully accessed systems at Hugging Face and an Australian government website. The monitoring framework Open A I published is meant to show the world it is taking that kind of unexpected behavior seriously.

Anthropic has filed its prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission to go public on the Nasdaq. Analysts who have read the document describe it as an aggressive bet on artificial general intelligence. The filing essentially frames every dollar Anthropic spends on compute as necessary infrastructure for building transformative artificial intelligence, and asks Wall Street to price the company accordingly. The debut could come within weeks.

Google's Gemini assistant gained meaningful new capabilities yesterday. Users can now generate, preview, and edit Google Docs, Google Sheets, slide decks, Markdown files, plain text files, and code files directly inside a Gemini conversation. This brings Gemini much closer to functioning as a full document workspace, not just a chat interface.

Microsoft, while expanding on the artificial intelligence front, is simultaneously making significant cuts to its workforce. The company is offering voluntary buyouts to roughly sixty thousand employees across the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and other markets. Satya Nadella has been signaling this direction for some time, but this week marks the clearest concrete step yet. The message from leadership is that artificial intelligence is reshaping what a software company needs in terms of headcount.

Reddit's stock fell sharply after the company posted weak sales guidance and revealed plans to begin testing a mandatory account login requirement. The concern driving the selloff is that Google search traffic to Reddit has been declining, and that traffic is a significant driver of Reddit's advertising revenue. The platform is responding by trying to push more users into logged-in sessions, but investors are not yet convinced that strategy closes the gap.

For markets broadly: Nasdaq futures are pointing lower this morning, weighed down by Intel and Amazon. The S and P five hundred snapped a three-day winning streak yesterday after Alphabet sold off on earnings. The earnings season continues to sort winners from losers very quickly, and the macro tone remains sensitive to any guidance that misses expectations.

That is the news. Now, the For You section.

First, a significant regulatory signal on peptides. The Department of Health and Human Services has officially green-lit six peptide compounds for compounding pharmacy use, at least on a temporary basis. This policy came over objections from career scientists at the Food and Drug Administration, who raised concerns about the evidence base for these compounds. The six peptides cleared include some that have become widely discussed in longevity and performance research circles. What this means in plain language is that certain peptide products that were in a legal gray area can now be legally compounded and dispensed through licensed pharmacies, at least until the policy is reviewed or reversed. This is not a full approval, and it is not a blanket safety endorsement. But it changes access meaningfully for people who have been trying to work with physicians to obtain these compounds. If you have had conversations with a doctor about peptide-based protocols, this policy shift is worth bringing up at your next appointment.

Second, your artificial intelligence move for today. Open A I's newly published monitoring framework for detecting model misalignment is genuinely worth reading, not as a technical deep dive, but as a practical lens for anyone deploying artificial intelligence agents in a professional context. Here is the specific thing to do today: go to Open A I's website and read the summary of their layered monitoring approach, then write down three points in your own workflow where an artificial intelligence agent you use could take an action you did not intend, or access something you did not expect. That exercise, which takes about twenty minutes, is the foundation for building a proper human checkpoint into any agentic workflow you are running. It is the kind of thinking that separates teams who use artificial intelligence well from teams that have a costly surprise six months from now.

Third, where to look in investing. Amazon Web Services growing at thirty nine percent while the broader company disappoints on free cash flow tells you something important: cloud infrastructure spending is accelerating, but the companies running that infrastructure are spending even faster to build it out. That dynamic, massive revenue growth alongside negative or shrinking free cash flow, is now a pattern across Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta. The companies that supply the hardware, the networking, and the software tooling for those data centers are where some investors are looking for the secondary beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence buildout, without directly owning the hyperscalers themselves. The risk is straightforward: if artificial intelligence monetization takes longer than expected to materialize at scale, capital expenditure spending gets cut first, and suppliers feel that immediately.
