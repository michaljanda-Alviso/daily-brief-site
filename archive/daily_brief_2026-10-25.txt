Now to artificial intelligence more broadly.

A new study out this week delivered a striking finding. When researchers forced two leading large language models to pick candidates in nearly five hundred election races, Grok, backed by Elon Musk's xAI, chose Republican candidates in the overwhelming majority of cases. Open A I's G P T leaned Democratic. The two systems disagreed in more than ninety percent of races tested. This is not a proof of conspiracy. It reflects how training data and reinforcement choices bake in subtle preferences over time. But as midterm voting begins across the United States, the finding lands with particular weight. These models are answering millions of political questions every day.

Pennsylvania's governor signed a new law this week requiring disclosures on artificial intelligence generated advertisements. Any ad, whether image, video, audio, or text, that has been significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence must now carry a label in the same format as the ad itself. Pennsylvania becomes one of the more significant states to put this kind of rule into law, and given the timing ahead of a midterm election, the signal is clear: lawmakers are no longer willing to wait for the technology industry to self-regulate on this one.

First, a health signal worth understanding.

Researchers published a fresh systematic review this week looking at what G L P one receptor agonists, the class of drugs that includes semaglutide and its relatives, actually do to bone health in adults with overweight or obesity. The findings are nuanced and worth knowing. While these drugs drive meaningful weight loss, and weight loss generally helps metabolic health, some evidence suggests that rapid fat loss without maintained muscle mass may reduce bone mineral density over time. The practical takeaway is this: if you or someone you care about is on a G L P one therapy, the conversation with a doctor should now include bone density monitoring and adequate protein intake to preserve lean muscle. This is not a reason to avoid these medications. Their cardiovascular and metabolic benefits are substantial. But the full picture of long-term skeletal effects is still being built, and patients deserve to be informed participants in that conversation.

Second, your artificial intelligence move for today.

Yesterday's study showing that G P T and Grok made sharply different political choices in the same election scenarios is more than a headline. It is a practical reminder that the large language model you use is not neutral. Here is what to do with that today. Run a simple bias audit on the tool you rely on most. Pick three topics relevant to your work or industry, and ask your usual model its opinion or recommendation. Then take the same questions to a competing model and compare the responses side by side. You do not need to switch tools. The goal is to know where your model leans so you can calibrate accordingly. This habit, running parallel queries on high-stakes questions, is a professional skill that will separate careful artificial intelligence users from credulous ones. Do it today, and make it a monthly practice.

Third, where to look in investing.

Biotech is having a moment. This week's European Society for Medical Oncology congress in Madrid delivered several high-profile clinical readouts, and the one generating the most conversation is a five-year follow-up from the Relativity zero four seven melanoma trial. The combination of two immune checkpoint drugs, relatlimab and nivolumab, produced statistically significant improvements in survival rates for patients with high levels of a specific biological marker called L A G three. That kind of durable survival data is the foundation on which biotech valuations are built. The broader sector has been rallying as mergers, acquisitions, and genuine clinical wins accumulate. Exchange traded funds tracking diversified biotech baskets give exposure without the binary risk of a single drug company's trial result. That said, biotech remains one of the more volatile corners of the market. A single failed trial can cut a company's value in half in a session. Diversification within the sector is not optional, it is the strategy.
