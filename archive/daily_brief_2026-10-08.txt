On the artificial intelligence safety side, a startup called Goodfire is taking a different approach to keeping artificial intelligence agents from going off the rails. Most existing oversight tools work by deploying a second artificial intelligence system to read and evaluate everything the first artificial intelligence does. That approach is expensive and slow. Goodfire says its new monitoring system works from the inside out, literally peeking inside the model as it runs and only flagging behavior when something looks genuinely anomalous. The company claims this cuts oversight costs significantly. In a world where enterprises are deploying dozens or hundreds of artificial intelligence agents simultaneously, cost-efficient monitoring is not a nice-to-have. It is a scaling requirement. Goodfire's approach, if it holds up, represents a meaningful shift in how artificial intelligence agent governance gets built.

First, a note on peptides and health research. There is a meaningful development worth flagging in the context of weight management science. Researchers have been examining what happens after people stop taking glucagon-like peptide one therapies, the class of drugs that includes medications like semaglutide. A recently circulated pilot randomized controlled trial looked specifically at strategies to prevent weight regain once someone discontinues that therapy. The findings suggest that abrupt discontinuation without a structured behavioral or metabolic support plan leads to rapid and significant weight regain in most participants. The researchers piloted several bridging protocols, including dietary structure, increased protein intake, and resistance training programming, and found that the combination approach outperformed any single intervention. The takeaway in plain language is this: glucagon-like peptide one drugs appear to do a substantial amount of the metabolic and appetite regulation work that people then lose when they stop taking them. The body's baseline hunger and metabolic rate tend to revert. For anyone in or near this conversation, either personally or as someone advising others, the research reinforces that exit planning matters as much as the treatment itself. Talk to a physician before making any changes, but be aware that the science is actively developing around long-term maintenance strategies.

Second, your artificial intelligence move for today. Given Goodfire's launch and the broader conversation around artificial intelligence agent oversight, here is something concrete you can do right now. If you are already using any artificial intelligence agent or automation tool in your workflow, whether that is an assistant that handles email drafts, a coding agent, or a research pipeline, take thirty minutes today to deliberately stress-test it. Give it an ambiguous instruction, one that could reasonably be interpreted in two very different ways, and watch what it does. Does it ask for clarification? Does it make a silent assumption? Does it produce an output that technically satisfies the prompt but misses your intent? Document what you observe. This is not just a productivity exercise. It is building your own mental model of where your agents need guardrails, which is exactly the problem Goodfire is solving at scale. The companies that deploy artificial intelligence reliably will be the ones whose teams understand agent failure modes from the inside, not just from the marketing deck. Thirty minutes today, one ambiguous prompt, and write down what you learn.
