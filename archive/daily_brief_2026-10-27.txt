Let us start with the biggest story out of Silicon Valley this morning, because it touches nearly everything else in the market today. Anthropic has officially filed its initial public offering prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission, planning to list on the Nasdaq exchange under the ticker symbol A N T. The company behind the Claude artificial intelligence assistant is laying out an ambitious and, in places, sobering picture of where it stands. More than half of its customers spending one hundred thousand dollars or more per year now use both its platform and at least one of its first-party applications. That cross-selling strategy has made enterprise customers noticeably stickier, and analysts are comparing the playbook to how Amazon built loyalty across its business lines. But the filing is also unusually candid about risk. Anthropic openly warns investors of what it calls catastrophic risk to humankind from the technology it is building. It also acknowledges a hard truth about powering that technology: data centers are becoming increasingly reliant on natural gas. The energy dependency is written into the risk section in plain language. With the artificial intelligence trade under pressure in recent weeks and interest rates still elevated, this public offering is being watched far beyond the tech sector. Analysts are calling it a trickle-down moment. If Anthropic prices well and holds, it could breathe confidence back into the broader technology rally. If it stumbles, the reverberations will be felt across the space.

First, on the health research front. The story worth your attention this morning comes from the European Society for Medical Oncology meeting this weekend. Merck, working alongside China's Kelun-Biotech, presented Phase Three trial results showing that their antibody-drug conjugate therapy cut the risk of death by one-third in patients with advanced endometrial cancer. Antibody-drug conjugates are a class of targeted therapy that essentially deliver a chemotherapy payload directly to cancer cells while leaving healthy tissue largely alone. This precision approach is part of a broader research direction showing that highly targeted cancer treatment continues to outperform broad chemotherapy in specific tumor types. The results do not involve peptides directly, but they reinforce the larger research signal: the more precisely a therapy can identify and bind to a biological target, the better the outcomes tend to be. That targeting logic runs through the entire peptide therapeutics field as well. If you follow precision oncology, this data set from the Merck trial is worth reading in full.

Second, for your artificial intelligence move today. Open A I now has multiple models in production with meaningfully different capabilities. G P T six Astra for complex workflow analysis, G P T six point one Sol for deep security review, and Codex Ultrafast for rapid incident investigation. Most people are still using a single model as a general assistant. Today's specific action: if you have a workflow that involves both broad reasoning and a narrower technical check, try splitting it. Use one model for the high-level analysis and route the specific technical or security component to a model optimized for that task. Greptile, a code review company, is doing exactly this in production, running Astra for user workflow testing and a separate model for security hardening. You do not have to be a software company to apply the same logic. Think about where your current single-model workflow has two genuinely different jobs hidden inside it, and experiment with splitting them today.
