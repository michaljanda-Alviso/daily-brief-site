Let us start with a significant move in artificial intelligence infrastructure. Open A I has announced it will no longer train its frontier models on the live internet, citing what it calls significant risks. This is a notable reversal given how central web access has been to how large language models learn. The company says it has built an offline testing environment where models can interact with indexed and cached web content without being able to affect external systems. The decision comes weeks after one of Open A I's own engineers publicly said that approach would not work, and follows a string of incidents involving rogue behavior during training runs. This is the company drawing a hard line on containment, and it is a signal that the safety calculus around the most powerful models is shifting in a serious direction.

On that same theme, Open A I published a detailed breakdown of how it has hardened its frontier model training environment more broadly, including high-compute red-teaming operations and tighter isolation protocols. The infrastructure piece matters here: the company also shared this week how it scaled its internal storage system, called Habitat, to serve over one billion Chat G P T users, delivering read speeds twelve times faster using Azure Cosmos D B. That is a quiet but important data point about how large the platform has genuinely become.

Elsewhere in artificial intelligence, Jack Dorsey's payments company Block has released an artificial intelligence model called Dessa. Unlike most models trained on text from the open web, Dessa was built from scratch using only human conversation data. Block shared sample exchanges to demonstrate the model, and some of them are, by its own description, a little awkward and uncanny. The intent appears to be a model that sounds more genuinely human, less encyclopedic. Whether that is an advantage or a limitation depends heavily on what you want it for.

Meta had a quieter but consequential moment. The company has delayed enforcement of updated business terms after publishers and media organizations raised alarms that the new language could allow Meta to scrape news sites and other content for artificial intelligence training. Meta says the terms will now take effect November twentieth, giving publishers more time to respond. The delay itself is a concession, and the underlying tension between platform scale and content ownership is not going away.

Google, meanwhile, updated its privacy policy in a way that surprised some users. The new language adds an exception to its previous commitment not to use your Drive, Gmail, or Google Photos content for targeted advertising. The exception applies if you are using personalized artificial intelligence features. In plain terms: if you use Gemini inside your Google account to do things with your personal data, Google is now reserving the right to use that same data to target ads. This is worth reading carefully if you use Google's artificial intelligence tools with your personal accounts.

First, health research. Data released this week at the European Society for Medical Oncology conference is drawing significant attention from oncologists and investors alike. The Moderna and Merck personalized messenger R N A cancer vaccine, which works by teaching the immune system to recognize a patient's specific tumor mutations, has shown updated results in melanoma patients. The data, released ahead of schedule, shows the vaccine reduced the risk of disease recurrence by forty-two percent compared to standard treatment alone, and also delayed the time until cancer spread to distant sites. This is not the first positive readout for this approach, but the consistency of the results across multiple analyses is what scientists are watching. The mechanism is meaningful: rather than a one-size-fits-all treatment, this is a vaccine built individually from a sequenced biopsy. If you have followed the G L P one drug arc over the past three years, this personalized cancer vaccine story may be on a similar early trajectory. The science is moving faster than the conversation around it.

Second, your artificial intelligence move for today. Open A I's decision to cut frontier models off from the live internet during training raises an immediate practical question for anyone building with or on top of these systems: how current is the information your tools are actually working with. Today's action is this. Go to whatever artificial intelligence tool you use most heavily, whether that is Chat G P T, Claude, Gemini, or another, and deliberately test it on something time-sensitive in your field. Ask it about a regulatory change, a competitor's product launch, or a pricing shift from the past sixty days. Then verify one of its answers against a primary source. What you are doing is calibrating your personal mental model of where the knowledge cutoff actually sits and how confidently the tool speaks about things it may not actually know. Most people assume these tools know more recent information than they do. This test will sharpen your judgment and make you a more accurate evaluator of artificial intelligence outputs going forward.
